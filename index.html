Why societies cooperate, innovate, and prosper

Using historical and cross-cultural evidence, I study how kinship and social structure shape institutions, psychology, and economic development.

I am Associate Professor of Economics at George Mason University, where I direct the undergraduate PPE program. I am Co-PI of the Historical Psychology Project at Harvard. In 2023, I received the Royal Economic Society Prize for my work on kinship and political institutions.

Economic Journal · 2022

RES Prize
Kin Networks and Institutional Development

On the Church, intensive kin networks, and the formation of participatory institutions and democracy in Europe.

One of the best papers on the subject.— Joel Mokyr

paper ↗ data ↗
Strong kin networks are associated with weaker democratic traditions and lower contemporary democracy. Historical evidence points to a mechanism: the Western Church's marriage regulations weakened clan-based kinship, while European regions with weaker kin networks were more likely to develop medieval communes and other participatory institutions. The paper received the 2022 Royal Economic Society Prize for the best paper published in The Economic Journal.

Science · 2019

The Church, Intensive Kinship, and Global Psychological Variation

With Duman Bahrami-Rad, Jonathan Beauchamp, and Joseph Henrich

On how the medieval Church reshaped family organization—and, over centuries, Western psychology.

paper ↗ data ↗ media ↓
Across countries, European regions, and individuals from different cultural backgrounds, longer exposure to the medieval Western Church and weaker intensive kinship are associated with greater individualism and impersonal prosociality and with less conformity. The paper links these patterns to the Church's historical transformation of marriage and family organization.

Journal of Political Economy · 2026

How Cultural Diversity Drives Innovation: Surnames and Patents in U.S. History

With Max Posch and Joseph Henrich

On why culturally diverse places can be more innovative: new combinations of ideas, skills, and breakthrough inventions in U.S. history.

paper ↗ data ↗ media ↓
Using U.S. data from 1850–1940 and quasi-random variation in counties' surname composition generated by historical immigration, we find that greater cultural diversity increased patenting and breakthrough innovation. The evidence points to a broader pool of ideas and skills for recombination and to social interactions that facilitate idea sharing.

Nature · 2016

Intrinsic Honesty and the Prevalence of Rule Violations across Societies

With Simon Gächter

On whether living in societies with widespread rule-breaking changes people's willingness to be honest—even when cheating cannot be detected.

paper ↗ data ↗ media ↓
In experiments with 2,568 participants across 23 countries, subject pools from societies with more corruption, tax evasion, and political fraud exhibited lower intrinsic honesty. The results are consistent with cultural co-evolution between institutions, social environments, and individual values.

Nature · 2018

The Moral Machine Experiment

With Edmond Awad, Sohan Dsouza, Richard Kim, Joseph Henrich, Azim Shariff, Jean-François Bonnefon, and Iyad Rahwan

On the moral choices societies want autonomous vehicles to make—and how those choices differ across cultures.

paper ↗ media ↓
Using nearly 40 million decisions from participants in 233 countries and territories, the study maps broad moral preferences in unavoidable accident scenarios and documents substantial cross-cultural variation in how those preferences are weighted.

Common Incentives, Unequal Gains: Global Evidence from Public-Goods Experiments

With Simon Gächter and Christian Thöni · CeDEx Discussion Paper

Across more than 40 societies, voluntary cooperation in public-goods experiments is positively associated with institutional quality. Laboratory experiments provide complementary causal evidence: experiencing well-functioning institutions subsequently increases voluntary cooperation even after formal enforcement is removed.

Cultural Evolution in Economics

With Nathan Nunn and Joseph Henrich · in preparation for Annual Review of Economics

Economics increasingly treats preferences, beliefs, and institutions not as primitives but as products of culture — socially transmitted information that evolves over time. We review the exchange between economics and the field of cultural evolution. Cultural evolution offers a formal framework that provides microfoundations for endogenous preferences, beliefs, and heuristics that economic models often take as given. These foundations help understand the emergence of social norms and institutions and their co-evolution with psychology, while explaining both cultural persistence and change. Economics contributes empirical strategies and a large body of evidence for identifying culture's causal footprint—natural experiments, discontinuity and epidemiological designs, and experimental methods in the field and laboratory. This line of research helps explain phenomena that standard models alone cannot: deviations from rational choice, belief systems such as religion and zero-sum thinking, why similar institutions perform differently across societies, and the cultural roots of innovation, growth, and the wealth of nations.
Beliefs That Bind: Religion and Shared Moral Order

With Firoz Ahmed and Andreas Pondorfer

Scholars have long argued that religion helps sustain social order by binding individuals into a shared moral community. Yet this coordinating role has rarely been tested directly. We test it in a lab-in-the-field experiment with 720 participants in rural Bangladesh. Participants are randomly exposed to a moralizing Islamic figure (Pir), a traditional supernatural agent (Gunin), or neither. We measure honesty and elicit both personal moral views and injunctive norms—participants’ beliefs about how acceptable others in their community consider dishonesty. Making the Pir salient increases honesty and strongly shifts injunctive norms, while having little effect on participants’ own moral views. It thereby closes about 40 percent of the pluralistic-ignorance gap; the Gunin has no comparable effects. Reanalyzing published data from 15 diverse societies and religions, we find further evidence that the coordinating role of moralizing religion extends beyond our experimental setting. Our findings provide evidence that moralizing religion can align communities around common moral expectations, helping sustain a shared moral order.
Intuitive Cooperation in Children across Societies

With Y. Dunham, E. Mandelbaum, K. McAuliffe, and D. Rand

We investigate whether the tendency to cooperate intuitively — without deliberation — varies systematically across societies and how this relates to cultural and institutional variation. Using a cross-cultural sample of children, we test whether cooperative intuitions are universal or culturally shaped.
